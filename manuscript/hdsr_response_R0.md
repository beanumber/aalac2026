"The AI Paradox in Higher Education: Amplifying Experts, Stunting Novices"

## Screening comments to author

This is an essential area of research today and I would support inviting a full manuscript. I find the proposal’s distinction between 'producing impressive work' and 'developing the capacities of the person doing that work' particularly compelling, and it offers an empirical exploration of a perspective many have intuitively adopted. This offers a strong basis for defending the value of statistics and data science education without reducing it to preparation for employment or the ability to use AI effectively. The authors’ broader commitment to education as the development of people, rather than simply the production of artifacts, is very welcome.

In developing the proposal into a manuscript, I would encourage authors to avoid oversimplifying the concepts of “amplifying experts” and “stunting novices,” which currently reads as more categorical than I think the argument requires. Novice at what, and expert in which activities? Greater productivity, improved task performance, deeper understanding, and the development of independent judgment are not interchangeable outcomes, and all might also be moving targets in history -- how might they be getting redefined now in ways that complicate this kind of empirical study?

The authors should also clarify the relationship between the shared position developed through their workshop and the broader research literature. This question - what forms of pedagogy cultivate internalized understanding and which cultivate the capacity for action without understanding - has been important for education specialists for a long time and that literature could help clarify the concepts in question and the significance of the current moment.

I am particularly interested in the discussion of mentoring, peer relationships, and learning communities here. How might we create new forms of social relations around our technologies to preserve the forms of learning and understanding and capacity that are valued here? This could be developed into a significant contribution about the social conditions through which expertise develops, rather than only individual knowledge acquisition. Humanists and many education specialists emphasize that all knowledge and learning are social activities, and so we have to attend to how social relations are impacted by technology or might be cultivated in new ways around it. Here, I would encourage attention to institutional conditions as well as student choices: assessment incentives, workload, and access to human support may help explain the practices the authors describe.
